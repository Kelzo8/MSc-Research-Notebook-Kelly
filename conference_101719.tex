\begin{abstract}
    Online platforms increasingly host comments generated by bots or large language models alongside genuine human opinions. This mixture creates challenges for platform trust, moderation, and users' ability to judge authenticity. This paper examines the problem of distinguishing bot-generated comments from human comments on online platforms and compares traditional machine learning methods with transformer-based detectors in a supervised classification setting. The study focuses on short, informal online comments and evaluates both model families under identical data, preprocessing, and train-validation-test conditions. Traditional models will use interpretable term-frequency and stylistic features, while the transformer-based approach will learn contextual representations through fine-tuning. Performance will be assessed using accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score, with particular attention to false positives. The study aims to determine whether transformer-based models provide a meaningful advantage over simpler feature-based methods for realistic comment-level bot detection.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Online platforms are increasingly flooded with comments that are not written by humans but generated by bots or large language models.
What once were simple discussion threads now mix genuine opinions with automated praise, spam, and subtle persuasion.
This growing presence of machine-generated comments raises concerns for platform trust, moderation workloads, and users’ ability to judge what is authentic \cite{zaitsu_can_2024,li_mage_2024}.
Before any detector can be evaluated on accuracy or robustness, we need to understand how well different model families can separate bot-generated comments from human ones in the first place \cite{kumar_m_scalar_2024,ahmad_human_2026}.
In this study, bot-generated comments refer to comments produced automatically by software systems, including systems that use large language models. The classification target is the origin of the comment text rather than the identity of the account that posts it.

Recent literature shows that machine-generated text detection is now an active area of research, with approaches ranging from classical machine learning and stylometric analysis to transformer-based and ensemble models. 
Kumar et al. demonstrate that a RoBERTa ensemble can perform strongly in a shared-task setting, while Li et al. show that detection becomes more difficult in broader, 
more realistic settings where texts come from multiple domains and generation sources \cite{kumar_m_scalar_2024,li_mage_2024}. 
Wu et al.'s DetectRL benchmark further shows that detector reliability can vary with domain, model type, text length, human revision, and adversarial modification, meaning that strong scores on a single benchmark may not reflect real-world performance \cite{wu_detectrl_2024}.
At the comment level, Zaitsu et al. show that fake public comments generated by ChatGPT can be distinguished from human comments using stylometric features and classification methods \cite{zaitsu_can_2024}. 
Ahmad et al.'s recent survey also indicates that transformer-based detectors are currently among the strongest approaches in the wider machine-generated text detection literature \cite{ahmad_human_2026}.
Sharma's MSc thesis further demonstrates that ensemble-style pipelines combining TF-IDF features, RoBERTa embeddings, and Random Forest classification can improve machine-generated text detection, reinforcing the relevance of hybrid approaches for this project \cite{surbhi_exploring_nodate}.
Recent evaluations of AI text detection tools also show important limitations in reliability and fairness. Weber-Wulff et al. found that available detection tools were neither accurate nor reliable in many cases, while Pratama et al. reported accuracy-bias trade-offs that can disproportionately affect certain groups of writers \cite{weber-wulff_testing_2023,pratama_accuracy-bias_2025}.
Xiang et al. similarly identify adversarial paraphrasing and weak cross-domain generalisation as persistent challenges for current AI-text detection approaches \cite{noauthor_ai-generated_2026}.
Recent work also shows that generative AI can alter the quality, authenticity, and volume of social-media content, which reinforces the need to study detection in realistic platform settings \cite{moller_impact_2026}.
This strengthens the case for evaluating detectors in a comment-level setting, where short, informal, and context-dependent language makes the task more challenging than general machine-generated text detection.

However, the existing literature does not yet provide a clear comparison of traditional machine learning methods and transformer-based detectors specifically for bot-generated comments in realistic online-platform contexts. Much of the current work either focuses on general machine-generated text detection or on domain-specific datasets that may not fully reflect the short, informal, and varied nature of online comments. This creates a practical gap as well as a methodological one, because a detector that performs well on longer or more formal texts may behave differently when applied to short, context-dependent comments. In online-platform settings, detection models must also cope with informal language, conversational shortcuts, and limited surrounding context, which can reduce the reliability of benchmark results. For that reason, it is important to evaluate traditional machine learning and transformer-based detectors under the same comment-level conditions rather than assuming that results from broader machine-generated text tasks will transfer directly. This study contributes a focused comment-level comparison of traditional machine learning and transformer-based detectors, with the goal of identifying which approach is more effective in realistic online-platform settings. The comparison is also useful because it can show whether the added complexity of transformer-based models provides a meaningful advantage over simpler feature-based methods in this setting. The study will frame this comparison as a supervised classification task using labelled bot-generated and human-written comments, allowing the two model families to be evaluated under the same conditions.
This study aims to determine whether traditional machine learning classifiers or transformer-based models are more effective for detecting bot-generated comments in online-platform settings. It addresses this question through a supervised comparative evaluation of labelled bot-generated and human-written comments using both feature-based and transformer-based detection approaches. By testing both model families under the same conditions, the study seeks to identify which approach is more suitable for realistic comment-level bot detection and to clarify the trade-offs between performance, robustness, and practicality.
To make the comparison meaningful, both model families will be evaluated using the same labelled set of bot-generated and human-written comments and the same train--validation--test split. Traditional approaches will use interpretable text representations, such as term-frequency and stylistic features, with standard classifiers, while the transformer-based approach will learn contextual representations through fine-tuning. Performance will be assessed using accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score, with particular attention to false positives, because incorrectly classifying genuine human comments as bot-generated could undermine trust in a moderation system. This design is intended to distinguish gains caused by model choice from gains caused by differences in data preparation or evaluation conditions.
The intended contribution is a controlled comment-level comparison that links predictive performance with the practical costs of deploying automated authenticity checks on online platforms, including interpretability, computational complexity, and the consequences of false-positive decisions.
The remainder of this paper is structured as follows: Section II reviews related work on machine-generated text detection and clarifies the research gap addressed in this study. Section III presents the proposed comparative methodology, including the model families, data, and evaluation strategy. Section IV reports the analysis and experimental testing framework used to assess detector performance. Section V presents the results and discussion, and Section VI concludes the paper with final observations and directions for future work.
